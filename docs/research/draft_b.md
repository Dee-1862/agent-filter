## 0. PRD 1.4 fact-check (do this first; the PRD numbers are right but their context is wrong)

### 0.1 ConfAIde, "39% / 57%"
- Source: Mireshghallah et al., *Can LLMs Keep a Secret? Testing Privacy Implications of Language Models via Contextual Integrity Theory*, ICLR 2024 (Spotlight). https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17884 (full text opened: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.17884). [verified-in-source]
- Abstract says GPT-4 and ChatGPT "reveal private information in contexts that humans would not, 39% and 57% of the time". The Introduction (p.2) says it is "flow information to inappropriate actors 39% and 57% of the time **in Tier 4**, even though they are directly instructed to preserve privacy".
- **Correction / precision:** 39% and 57% are the *Leaks Secret* values for the **Tier 4 action-item task** (Table 4, second block: GPT-4 0.39, ChatGPT 0.57, mean over 10 runs). The **meeting-summary** task in the same table is lower: GPT-4 **0.29**, ChatGPT **0.38**. The abstract does not say which task. Worst-case over 10 runs is 0.80 (GPT-4) and 0.85 (ChatGPT) for both tasks.
- Other checked numbers (Table 4): Omits Public Information, action items: GPT-4 0.10, ChatGPT 0.27; summary: 0.76 and 0.89. Composite "Leaks Secret or Omits Info." for summary: 0.89 (GPT-4), 0.96 (ChatGPT). So leak rate alone hides a large utility loss. The PRD's "summary usefulness" metric is justified, and the PRD should quote the composite.
- Chain of thought: Table 5, Tier 4 Leaks Secret w/o CoT -> w/ CoT: GPT-4 0.39 -> 0.40, ChatGPT 0.57 -> 0.61. The text (Sec. 4.5) says CoT "does not improve leakage, in fact it makes the leakage more severe". This is the evidence the PRD leans on, and it is **superseded** by 2025 work (items 3, 4 below), where CI-style CoT does cut leakage on GPT-4o.
- Tier 4 construction (Sec. 3.4): **20 transcripts** generated by GPT-4, factorial 5 secrets x 4 public-information types; three people discuss a secret about a fourth person X, state that X should not know, then X joins. Neither secret nor public info is repeated. **Consequences for OTR-Bench:** (a) "20 from ConfAIde Tier 4" is the *entire* tier, not a sample; (b) the 20 items are not independent (5 x 4 grid, cluster by secret); (c) the secret is announced as confidential in the dialogue ("X should remain unaware"), so it is *explicit-in-context*, not the "implicit from content alone" case the PRD uses it for (health/salary with no cue). Label them "contextual-confidential" not "implicit".
- Leak metric caveat (Appendix B.4): authors read all 200 GPT-4 action-item responses and found **16 leaks missed by exact string matching** (e.g. secret "move to VISTA" vs response "transition to the VISTA project"), and no string-match false positives. String match under-counts leaks; for OTR-Bench use span-level paraphrase-aware judging.
- Design change: bench/run_eval should report Leak, Omit-Public, and Leak-or-Omit per ConfAIde, and 10 sampled runs per item for any stochastic LLM stage (ConfAIde did 10 runs; report mean and worst case).

### 0.2 PrivacyLens, "25.68% / 38.69%"
- Source: Shao, Li, Shi, Liu, Yang, *PrivacyLens: Evaluating Privacy Norm Awareness of Language Models in Action*, NeurIPS 2024 Datasets and Benchmarks. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00138 (full text opened: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.00138). [verified-in-source]
- Abstract: "GPT-4 and Llama-3-70B, leak sensitive information in 25.68% and 38.69% of cases, even when prompted with privacy-enhancing instructions."
- **Correction / precision (Table 2, privacy-enhancing prompt columns):** 25.68 is GPT-4's **adjusted** leakage rate **LRh** (raw LR = 24.54; basic prompt: LR 26.37, LRh 27.23). 38.69 is Llama-3-70B's **LRh** (raw LR = 39.15; basic prompt: LR 36.51, LRh 38.04). LRh = leakage among cases with positive helpfulness (Sec. 5.1). So the PRD should say "adjusted leakage rate".
- What it shows for our argument: privacy-enhancing prompt moves GPT-4 LRh 27.23 -> 25.68 and Llama-3-70B 38.04 -> 38.69 (slightly worse). Sec. 5.2: the privacy-enhancing prompt "improves probing results, it does not significantly boost performance in action-based evaluation"; models "may still leak information, despite considering privacy in their thought process".
- Scale and judge facts for our methodology: 493 seeds -> 493 vignettes -> 493 trajectories. Leak judge is a **few-shot Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2** classifier; validated on 153 (action, fact) pairs from 50 final actions labelled by 4 annotators: Fleiss' kappa 0.82 with human majority, accuracy 0.92. The *helpfulness* judge agreed far less: kappa **0.56**. Seed validation kappa 0.79.
- It is an **agent-action** benchmark (email, messenger), not transcript summarisation. It supports "prompting is not enough" for agents, but is indirect evidence for meetings.
- Design change: when we log judge agreement for OTR-Bench (Q4), copy this protocol: hand-label >= 100 (output, span) pairs, report kappa and accuracy of the local judge, and report leak rate with the judge's measured error (see Sec. 3 of the end matter).

## 1. Work that challenges "structure beats prompting"

### 1. Li, Sun, Guan, Zhou, Sap. *1-2-3 Check: Enhancing Contextual Privacy in LLM via Multi-Agent Reasoning* (CMU), arXiv:2508.07667v3, Feb 2026.
URL: https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07667 (also PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07667). Venue not stated on the page I opened; an earlier version appeared at the LLMSEC 2025 workshop (aclanthology 2025.llmsec-1.9, seen only in a search result). [verified-in-source]
- Claim: splitting meeting summarisation into Extractor / Checker / Executor agents cuts leakage on ConfAIde Tier 4.
- Numbers (Table 2, GPT-4o, leak % / omit-public % / composite %): Single agent 23.0 / 12.0 / 26.3. **Single agent + CoT 2.0 / 32.5 / 34.0.** Three agents, Annotate Private 5.0 / 20.0 / 24.5. Three agents, Annotate Private, no transcript to executor: 8.5 / 10.0 / **17.5** (best). LLaMA-3.1-70B: single 29.5 / 23.5 / 48.5; single+CoT 3.0 / 27.5 / 29.5; three agents Public Only 3.0 / 19.5 / **20.5** (best). Values carry +/- std over runs.
- **Threat to us (strong):** a plain CoT prompt on a 2025-class model gets Tier 4 leakage to 2-3%. ConfAIde's "CoT does not help" (2023) no longer holds. Our baseline B ("one LLM pass: remove anything off the record") is weaker than the best prompting baseline in the literature, so a win of C over B is partly a strawman win. The price of CoT is omission (32.5%), which is why the composite matters.
- **Support for us:** their best systems are *pipelines* (information-flow staged), not single prompts, and their own limitations section says the overhead "may be impractical" for "streaming meeting transcripts". This is the structure-vs-prompt question again, but inside the LLM; a staged LLM pipeline is a competitor to our state machine, not a baseline.
- Change -> pipeline part `bench/` systems and `otr/llm.py`: add **B+** = CI-aware CoT prompt (extract flows, judge each, then summarise) and run it in two modes (see Top-5 #1). Report leak, omit, and composite for A, B, B+, C.

### 2. Lan, Inan, Abdelnabi, Kulkarni, Wutschitz, Shokri, Brinton, Sim. *Contextual Integrity in LLMs via Reasoning and Reinforcement Learning*, NeurIPS 2025, arXiv:2506.04245.
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.04245 ; full text HTML https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04245v3 (I read the first ~100k of 160k characters; ConfAIde appendix was not in the portion read). [verified-in-source for the PrivacyLens table; ConfAIde claim abstract/secondhand]
- Claim: prompt the LLM to reason about CI, then RL-train on ~700 synthetic examples; leakage falls on PrivacyLens while helpfulness is roughly kept.
- Numbers (PrivacyLens table, flattened text, columns mapped by position, so treat the mapping as probable): LR % baseline -> CI-CoT prompt: Claude 3.7 Sonnet 30.4 -> 23.1; Gemini 2.5 Flash 29.0 -> 19.7; Claude 3.7 Sonnet Thinking 32.0 -> 20.1; Gemini 2.5 Pro 37.3 -> 25.3. Small open models (base -> CI-CoT -> CI-RL): Qwen2.5-7B 50.3 -> 44.8 -> 33.7; Qwen2.5-14B 52.9 -> 42.8 -> 33.9; Mistral-7B 47.9 -> 28.8 -> 31.2; Llama-3.1-8B 18.2 -> 21.3 -> 18.5 (no gain). Synthetic data split 590 / 66 / 73. Judge for PrivacyLens: GPT-4o 2024-11-20, temp 0. The abstract-level claim "up to 40%" is not visible as a single row in the table I read (closest: Qwen2.5-14B 52.9 -> 33.9, about 36% relative).
- **Threat:** two things at once. (i) Prompt-only CI reasoning already removes 7-11 points from frontier models. (ii) Even after RL, 7B-14B models leak 31-34%; frontier models with the CI prompt still leak 20-25%. So *no* approach (prompt or trained) is near zero on PrivacyLens, which supports the idea that a non-LLM safety layer has headroom.
- **For our local setup:** our LLM is `qwen2.5:3b/7b`. Qwen2.5-7B baseline LR is 50.3% here. The implicit-content check in C inherits that weakness. This is the most concrete reason the first no-LLM run (C leaks 45%) may not improve as much as hoped with a small local model.
- Change -> `otr/llm.py` and eval: report results for at least two LLM sizes (3B/7B local; one stronger model as an upper bound, clearly labelled non-local) to separate "structure helps" from "LLM too weak".

### 3. Wang, Yu et al. *Privacy in Action: Towards Realistic Privacy Mitigation and Evaluation for LLM-Powered Agents* (Microsoft, Wuhan U.), Findings of EMNLP 2025, arXiv:2509.17488 ("PrivacyChecker", "PrivacyLens-Live").
URL: https://arxiv.org/html/2509.17488v1 (HTML opened). [verified-in-source]
- Claim: a **prompting** method (extract each information flow as JSON: sender, recipient, subject, data type, transmission principle; judge each flow yes/no; drop or abstract the "no" flows) reduces leakage a lot with small helpfulness cost.
- Numbers (Table 1, PrivacyLens LR %, baseline -> PrivacyChecker): GPT-4o 33.06 -> 8.32 (helpfulness 2.69 -> 2.45); GPT-4.5 29.41 -> 9.55; DeepSeek-R1 36.08 -> 7.30; o1 27.24 -> 11.63; Qwen3-14B-T 36.11 -> 11.76; Qwen3-8B-NT 39.76 -> 24.20. Ablation (Table 2): no verbalisation of flows GPT-4o 11.36, Qwen3-14B-NT 30.63 vs 15.91 with it. Baseline observation: model labelled private info sensitive 98.0% of the time but still leaked in 33.1%. Failure analysis of DeepSeek-R1 residual leaks: 18 of 36 wrong judgment, 11 judge-action gaps, 3 missed flow extraction. Live (Table 3): leakage worse with more tools (6.7 -> 16.7 for 2 -> 3 tools); live samples ~150 MCP + 36 new cases (small).
- **Threat (strong):** the best-performing "structure" in the 2025 literature is *structured prompting in a single model*, not an external state machine. Extract-then-judge is exactly the "LLM context check" in our C; the question is whether the state machine and hold buffer add anything beyond a good extract-then-judge prompt run in a sliding window. It also says verbalising the flows is what matters (ablation), which hints that the gain comes from forcing explicit intermediate structure, a claim consistent with our hypothesis but in a different place.
- Change -> `otr/cue_detector.py`/`otr/llm.py`: have the implicit check emit explicit flow tuples (who said, about whom, data type, audience = everyone in notes) before judging. This is a cheap ablation: C-with-flows vs C-plain.

### 4. Fu, Qin, Zhang, Lin, Wutschitz, Sim, Rajmohan, Zhang. *CI-Work: Benchmarking Contextual Integrity in Enterprise LLM Agents*, ACL 2026 Industry Track, pp. 1483-1508.
URL: https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-industry.103.pdf (PDF downloaded and text-extracted). [verified-in-source]
- Claim: frontier models still violate CI in dense enterprise retrieval (explicitly including meeting transcripts as a data source); scaling and reasoning effort do not fix it.
- Numbers: abstract: "violation rates range from 15.8%-50.9%, with leakage reaching up to 26.7%"; "higher task utility often correlates with increased privacy violations"; "Simply increasing model size or reasoning depth fails to address the problem"; conclusion calls for "context-centric architectures". Table 2 (GPT-5): no defence LR 11.21 / violation rate 27.83 / conveyance rate 93.04; Prompt Defense 8.96 / 21.31 / 81.01; CI-CoT 8.95 / 22.13 / 84.90. Text: "even CI-CoT still yields a violation rate above 20% and reduces conveyance by 9%, indicating that prompt-level mitigation alone is far from sufficient." Disclaimer: synthetic data.
- **Mixed:** supports us (prompt-only leaves >20% violations on GPT-5; architecture recommended) but is not evidence about *our* architecture, and it is synthetic.
- Change: adopt **conveyance** (public content kept) as the utility twin of leak, as CI-Work and ConfAIde do; our "summary usefulness" is the same idea. Always plot leak vs conveyance.

### 5. Green, Gubri, Puerto, Yun, Oh. *Leaky Thoughts: Large Reasoning Models Are Not Private Thinkers*, EMNLP 2025 (Main), arXiv:2506.15674.
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.15674 [abstract-only]
- Claim: reasoning traces of reasoning models contain sensitive user data; more test-time compute makes final answers more cautious but traces more verbose and leaky.
- Threat/support: weakens "just use a reasoning model" as the fix, but only if traces are stored or exposed. For us, traces never reach the notes; the real risk is **logging**: if the audit log or debug output stores the LLM's reasoning containing the redacted content, we recreate the leak. No numbers in the abstract.
- Change -> `otr/state_machine.py` audit log: log span IDs and hashes, not redacted text; never persist reasoning traces; add a test that grep-ing all output/log files for planted secrets returns nothing.

### 6. Bagdasarian, Yi, Ghalebikesabi, Kairouz, Gruteser, Oh, Balle, Ramage. *AirGapAgent: Protecting Privacy-Conscious Conversational Agents*, CCS 2024, arXiv:2405.05175.
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05175 [abstract-only]
- Claim: architectural data minimisation (the agent only ever sees task-relevant data) beats giving the agent everything plus a prompt. Abstract: a single-query hijacking attack drops a Gemini Ultra agent's protection of user data from 94% to 45%; AirGapAgent keeps 97%.
- **Support (structure beats prompting):** closest evidence that *removal before the generator sees the data* is more robust than *instruction to the generator*. This maps directly onto our hold buffer: the summariser never receives held/redacted text. It is the strongest published analogue for our hypothesis, but it is about adversarial hijacking of an agent, not meeting speech.
- Change -> `otr/pipeline.py`: enforce the air-gap property as an invariant and test it (the summariser input is built only from committed segments; nothing else is in its context, including no "[REDACTED: topic]" markers that leak the topic).

### 7. Debenedetti, Shumailov, Fan, Hayes, Carlini, Fabian, Kern, Shi, Terzis, Tramer. *Defeating Prompt Injections by Design* (CaMeL), arXiv:2503.18813.
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18813 [abstract-only]
- Claim: wrapping the LLM in a system layer with separated control/data flow and capability checks gives "provable security" on 77% of AgentDojo tasks vs 84% for an undefended system.
- Support, by analogy only: security-critical decisions should not be left to the model's own compliance; a small utility cost buys guarantees. Our analogue: deterministic path for explicit cues; LLM only for ambiguity.
- Honest limit: this is prompt injection, not privacy norm judgement. Deterministic guarantees do not cover "is this sentence sensitive?", which is exactly the part that needs an LLM.
- Change: argue in the writeup that structure gives guarantees for the *explicit* and *scope* cases only, and falls back on LLM accuracy for implicit ones. Report per-type, never a single blended number.

### 8. Ruzzetti, Emde, Yun, Oh, Gubri. *MuPPET: A Benchmark for Contextual Privacy of LLM Assistants in Multi-Party Conversations*, arXiv:2606.23217 (Jun 2026).

- Relevance: the nearest benchmark to a meeting with many listeners. Threat: our per-speaker streams may help, since we know who is speaking, but the audience (everyone who will read the notes) is the whole room. No numbers seen. Check whether its data is reusable for OTR-Bench "implicit" items (license/format not verified).

### 9. Gupta et al. *PiSAs: Benchmarking Contextual Integrity in Multi-User Agentic Systems*, arXiv:2607.05318 (v1 Jul 2026, v2 Oct 2026).

### 12. Machine unlearning / "right to be forgotten" literature (Yao et al. arXiv:2402.15159; Unlearning at Scale arXiv:2508.12220; survey arXiv:2510.25117).
URLs seen only as search results (not opened). [secondhand]

### 13. Disfluency and repair: Chen, Zayats, Walker, Padfield, *Teaching BERT to Wait: Balancing Accuracy and Latency for Streaming Disfluency Detection*, NAACL 2022, arXiv:2205.00620; Hough and Purver, *Strongly Incremental Repair Detection* (STIR), arXiv:1408.6788.

- Change -> `bench/` type 3: add paired items, retraction vs repair; and a PENDING_CONFIRM path for ambiguous "strike that". The scale of the problem (how often speakers use "strike that" as repair vs privacy) is **unverified**; no corpus numbers found.

## 3. Streaming ASR + diarization numbers that bound the hold buffer

### 14. Machacek, Dabre, Bojar. *Turning Whisper into Real-Time Transcription System*, IJCNLP-AACL 2023 demo, arXiv:2307.14743 (the `whisper_streaming` / LocalAgreement paper).
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14743 (full PDF opened). [verified-in-source]
- Claim: "3.3 seconds latency on average for English ASR on the European Parliament" speeches (abstract, 1 s MinChunkSize in the text: "average latency is 3.3 seconds for English, 4.4 seconds for German and 4.8 seconds for Czech, while the WER is by 2% higher than offline for English and German, and by 6% higher for Czech").
- Numbers behind it: Table 1 (one 3:36 document, 10 repeated runs, MinChunkSize 0.1 s): WER 5.8 +/- 0.9, latency 2.85 +/- 0.45 s on A40 without VAD; 3.12 +/- 0.36 with VAD; on L40 3.58 +/- 0.62 / 3.96 +/- 0.81. Standard deviation of latency "from 0.36 to 0.81 seconds" from uncontrollable compute load. Across setups, "average latency between 0 and 16.1 seconds" (excluding one outlier document). Audio buffer trimmed at about 30 s. Latency definition: time between emission of a word and when the aligned gold word was spoken. Table 3 layout in my extraction is garbled (the 3.3 s appears on the 0.1 s row of one table and in the text for 1 s), so quote the text claim, not the table cell.
- Caveats: GPU (A40/L40, 48 GB), fluent parliamentary speech, single speaker, no overlap. **No laptop CPU figure.** Our Q1/Q3.
- Bound for hold buffer: ASR confirmation alone costs about 3-5 s on a server GPU (more on a laptop, unmeasured by me). So confirmed text is already 3-5 s behind speech. A hold of 30-60 s is a *policy* number (how long after a sentence do people say "strike that"), not an ASR number. I found no corpus measurement of retraction delay. This is an open question (Q-new-2).
- Change -> `otr/hold_buffer.py`: define hold relative to *speech time*, not arrival time, and log both ASR confirmation delay and hold delay so P95 end-to-end delay can be reported. Report the sweep (F5) as end-to-end delay = ASR + hold.

### 15. Medennikov et al. (NVIDIA). *Streaming Sortformer: Speaker Cache-Based Online Speaker Diarization with Arrival-Time Ordering*, arXiv:2507.18446.
URL: https://arxiv.org/html/2507.18446 (tables extracted from HTML). Authors: first author Medennikov (from PDF metadata), others not confirmed. [verified-in-source]
- Table 1, DER % (collar 0 s DIHARD III; 0.25 s CALLHOME), Streaming Sortformer-AOSC with post-processing: latency 10 s: DIHARD III all 19.02; 1.04 s: **18.97**; 0.32 s: **19.32**. Without post-processing, 1.04 s: 19.89 and 0.32 s: 20.25. CALLHOME-part2 all-speakers column: 11.22 (10 s) / 12.40 (1.04 s) / 13.64 (0.32 s). LiveKit gives per-participant tracks, so **we should not need diarization for the live path**; diarization matters only for mixed audio (WORK_SPLIT task 6).
- Change: score cue/scope handling with oracle speaker labels *and* with diarized labels, so the effect of attribution error is a measured column, not a guess.

### 16. Aperdannier, Schacht, Piazza. *Systematic Evaluation of Online Speaker Diarization Systems Regarding their Latency*, arXiv:2407.04293 (Jul 2024).

### 18. SimulStreaming (Machacek, Polak; CUNI IWSLT 2025 submission), arXiv:2506.17077.

## 4. Evaluation methodology for ~40 items

### 20. Miller. *Adding Error Bars to Evals: A Statistical Approach to Language Model Evaluations*, arXiv:2411.00640 (Nov 2024).
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640 (abstract opened; full PDF text-extracted). [verified-in-source]
- Recommendations as listed in Sec. 1 of the paper: report standard errors (Bernoulli SE = sqrt(p(1-p)/n), Eq. 2); **cluster standard errors** when questions come in related groups (Eq. 4; Table 4 shows clustered SEs "up to 3X" larger on real evals; Table 3 suggests reporting cluster count with question count); reduce variance by resampling answers; compare models with **paired differences** (Sec. 4.2, Eq. 7) rather than unpaired summaries; use **power analysis**.
- Application: our 20 Tier-4 items are a 5 x 4 grid (5 secrets x 4 public-info types) -> cluster by secret (5 clusters) or by source transcript. QMSum/AMI-based items share source meetings -> cluster by source. State cluster counts next to n.

### 21. Bowyer, Aitchison, Ivanova. *Position: Don't Use the CLT in LLM Evals With Fewer Than a Few Hundred Datapoints*, ICML 2025 (PMLR 267), arXiv:2503.01747.
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01747 (PDF text-extracted: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.01747). [verified-in-source]
- Claim: with small N the CLT interval "dramatically underestimat[es] uncertainty", can extend beyond [0,1] or collapse to zero (Figure 1, N = 20 questions); for IID questions, **Wilson and Bayesian** intervals reach nominal coverage at small N (Figure 2, N = 3, 10, 30, 100), while CLT and **bootstrap** show poor calibration (actual coverage below nominal); 95% CLT intervals gave only 92.5% coverage at N = 100 in the clustered setting (Sec. 3.2). Python Bayesian library: github.com/sambowyer/bayes_evals.
- Application: use Wilson (or exact Clopper-Pearson) for each leak rate; use exact paired tests for system comparisons; do not use percentile bootstrap as the headline at n = 40.

## Strongest objections to our hypothesis (ordered)

1. **Strawman baseline.** B is "one LLM pass: remove anything off the record". 2025 work shows CI-style prompting on GPT-4o drives ConfAIde Tier 4 leakage from 23.0% to 2.0% (item 1, Table 2) and PrivacyChecker's extract-then-judge cuts PrivacyLens leakage 33.06% -> 8.32% (item 3). A fair "prompting alone" arm is B+ (CI-CoT or flow extraction), and the claim must be "C beats B+ at matched latency", not "C beats a naive prompt".
2. **Hindsight advantage / unequal information.** An offline single pass sees the whole transcript, including a later "strike that". Retroactive cases are then trivial for B and the advantage of C is *online operation under a delay budget*, not leak rate per se. Compare B in both full-hindsight and causal-with-lag modes; otherwise the headline is confounded.
3. **The structure may only move error.** In the first run, C leaks 45% vs A 14% (WORK_SPLIT). The state machine adds new failure modes (scope closes early, I1; false triggers, I3; "strike that" as repair, I6). Published staged pipelines (item 1) gain from LLM stages, not from rule structure. If the gain comes from the LLM check, "structure" is not what we should credit.
4. **Our LLM is the weak link.** Local 3B/7B models are the least capable setting in the literature (Qwen2.5-7B baseline LR 50.3% on PrivacyLens, item 2; PiSAs and PrivacyChecker both name LLM judgement as the bottleneck). The implicit-content arm may cap out regardless of structure.
5. **The benchmark cannot show what we want.** 40 items, 20 of them the full ConfAIde Tier 4 grid that is explicit-in-context and clustered; synthetic insertion by an LLM that is also our judge family; per-type cells of 4-8 items. See honest reporting below: even a 30% -> 12.5% drop is not significant under an exact paired test at n = 40.
6. **Leak metric gameability.** Leak rate falls to 2% by omitting public content (CoT: omits 32.5%). Without the omit/conveyance twin the primary metric rewards over-redaction.
7. **ASR errors cap cue recall** and Whisper hallucinates about 1% of transcriptions (item 19). Cue detector tests on clean text overstate performance.
8. **Legal framing is not solved by suppression** (item 23): consent to record is a separate limb; local processing is our protection, not the state machine.

## Top 5 changes (each mapped to the pipeline)

1. **Strong baselines, matched information (bench/run_eval, otr/llm.py).** Add B+ (CI-CoT / extract-flows-then-judge). Run B and B+ in (i) offline-hindsight and (ii) causal streaming with the same hold lag as C. Primary claim: C vs best of B+ in the causal mode.
2. **Report leak and conveyance together (bench metrics).** Per ConfAIde Table 4 and CI-Work Table 2: Leak, Omit-Public (over-redaction), Leak-or-Omit composite, plus a leak-vs-hold-length curve (F5). Add worst-case over k sampled runs for any stochastic LLM stage (ConfAIde used 10).
3. **Replace string-match-only judging (bench).** Gold private spans per item, paraphrase-aware judge, and a human-labelled validation set of >= 100 (output, span) pairs with kappa and accuracy reported (PrivacyLens protocol: 153 pairs, kappa 0.82). Hand-label all 40 items' gold spans, not 10 (WORK_SPLIT task 5 says ~10).
4. **Stress the front end (bench, otr/cue_detector.py, otr/hold_buffer.py).** ASR-noise condition for cue phrases; repair-vs-retraction pairs for "strike that"; PII across segment boundaries; define hold relative to speech time; log ASR delay + hold delay.
5. **Privacy-of-the-privacy-layer invariants (otr/pipeline.py, otr/state_machine.py, otr/ingest).** Summariser input is committed-only (air gap); audit log stores IDs/hashes, never redacted text or reasoning traces; removal cascades to derived summaries with provenance and regeneration; no speaker embeddings persisted; speaker identity from LiveKit participant, not voice.

## How to report results honestly with 40 items (worked method)

**Unit of analysis:** the item (one snippet), outcome = leaked (1) or not (0), where "leaked" = any gold private span recoverable from the output by the validated judge. Span-level counts are secondary and need item-clustered inference.

**Step 1: single-system rate with a Wilson 95% interval** (not +/- 1.96 sqrt(p(1-p)/n); Bowyer et al. show the CLT interval is too narrow at small n). Wilson: centre = (p + z^2/2n)/(1 + z^2/n), half-width = z sqrt(p(1-p)/n + z^2/4n^2)/(1 + z^2/n), z = 1.96. Values I computed (n = 40): 
- 0/40 -> [0.0%, 8.8%] (never write "0% leakage"; write "0/40, upper 95% bound 8.8%"); exact Clopper-Pearson upper bound is also 8.8%. 
- 2/40 -> [1.4%, 16.5%]; 5/40 -> [5.5%, 26.1%]; 6/40 -> [7.1%, 29.1%]; 12/40 -> [18.1%, 45.4%]; 20/40 -> [35.2%, 64.8%].
- By case type with n = 4-8 per type, intervals span about 40-60 points. Report counts (k/n) and say "descriptive only".

**Step 2: paired comparison, exact McNemar.** Both systems run on the same 40 items. Build the 2 x 2 table of discordant items: b = B leaks and C does not, c = C leaks and B does not. Test H0: b ~ Binomial(b + c, 0.5), two-sided exact. Report b, c, and the paired risk difference (b - c)/n with an interval.
- *Worked example (invented counts for illustration, not our data):* B leaks 12/40 (30.0%, Wilson [18.1, 45.4]); C leaks 5/40 (12.5%, Wilson [5.5, 26.1]); discordant b = 9, c = 2 (3 items leaked in both). Exact McNemar p = 2 x P(X >= 9 | n = 11, p = 0.5) = **0.065** (not significant at 0.05). Risk difference = (9 - 2)/40 = **17.5 points**. A percentile bootstrap of the paired data gives [2.5, 32.5] points, which *excludes zero* even though the exact test does not: this is the small-n bootstrap anti-conservatism Bowyer et al. warn about, so do not headline the bootstrap. Honest sentence: "C leaked on 5/40 items (12.5%, 95% Wilson CI 5.5-26.1) vs 12/40 (30.0%, 18.1-45.4) for B; paired difference 17.5 points; exact McNemar p = 0.065; early evidence, not significant at the 0.05 level."
- *Power check:* for an unpaired two-proportion test, detecting 30% vs 10% at alpha = 0.05 with 80% power needs about 62 items per system (normal-approximation formula; my calculation). 40 items can only reliably detect very large effects (paired designs help, but not to that extent when discordance is low). Say so.

**Step 3: clustering.** Group items by source (the 5 Tier-4 secrets; each QMSum/AMI source meeting). Report the number of clusters next to n (Miller Table 3). Use a cluster-aware check (cluster bootstrap or cluster-level sign test) as a sensitivity analysis; Miller's clustered SEs were up to 3x larger than naive on real evals.

**Step 4: multiple stochastic runs.** If the LLM is sampled, run k >= 5 per item at fixed temperature, report mean leak per item then average across items, plus worst-case. Runs reduce sampling noise, not item-selection noise: do not treat k x 40 as 200 independent trials.

**Step 5: fix the analysis before looking.** Pre-register one primary comparison (C vs B+ causal, item-level leak, exact McNemar) and one co-primary utility measure (omit-public). Everything per-type is exploratory. Show the full leak-vs-omit table for A, B, B+, C and the hold sweep; do not drop A or B+ if they win on some type (WORK_SPLIT current table: A leaks less than C).

**Step 6: judge error.** Report judge sensitivity/specificity on the human-labelled set; show leak rate under "judge says leak" and under "human-labelled subset only". If the local judge's false-negative rate is unknown, say so; ConfAIde found 16/200 (8%) leaks missed by string matching.

**Step 7: language.** Use "early evidence", "in this 40-item synthetic set", never "outperforms" without the paired p-value and CI. Say which items were hand-checked (n) and that the insertions are LLM-generated.

## 5 open questions for PRD 2.6

1. **Q5 (new): What is B+?** Which strongest prompt baseline do we adopt (CI-CoT per Lan et al., extract-flows-then-judge per PrivacyChecker, three-agent per 1-2-3 Check), and does it run with hindsight or causal-with-lag? This decides whether the hypothesis is testable.
2. **Q6 (new): How long after a sentence do people retract it?** Hold buffer 30 s vs 60 s (Q3) is guessed. ASR confirmation is about 3-5 s on server GPU (item 14); retraction delay is unmeasured. Can we get it from real meeting data, or at least from a short human study (even 10 people, script variants)?
3. **Q4 (refined): Judge = what, validated how?** String match misses about 8% on ConfAIde (16/200); local 7B few-shot judge reached kappa 0.82 on PrivacyLens leak but 0.56 on helpfulness. Do we accept a local judge for the primary metric, or require a hand-labelled gold-span set for all 40 items (cheap, and removes judge error from the headline)?
4. **Q7 (new): Is "implicit" in OTR-Bench really implicit?** Tier 4 secrets are announced as confidential in context; true implicit cases (health/salary with no cue) must come from elsewhere. How do we label ground truth without circularity with the LLM that inserted them (extends Q2)?
5. **Q8 (new): What is the claim boundary on consent and biometrics?** Local-only and no-voiceprint are in the foundation; do we also document that suppression does not substitute for consent to record (Otter/Fireflies theories), and do we need an explicit consent roll call (F3) in scope for the demo narrative?
